# Business case

Easy Eats is an AI-based recipe app that helps users plan meals around their budgets, pantry items, available appliances, dietary restrictions, and local grocery deals. Users pick a budget (for example, $20 or under $100), and the app builds a weekly meal plan and grocery list that includes breakfast, lunch, and dinner. A swap chat answers "what can I use instead?" questions, and short tutorials teach beginners basic cooking skills.
The website is easy to copy, but every rating, swap review, and price report makes it smarter and more useful to the community.

## 1. The idea

- **Customer:** Easy Eats is for students at StFX who want to eat well for less and who have difficulty planning healthy meals, where to shop, and shopping on a budget. Easy Eats will start with a launch with StFX students first, then expand to groups like young adults, families, and people looking to cook once the community is established.
- **Problem:** Finding recipes is not easy. They can be hard to follow, do not cater to your budget, and do not help you cook with what you already own. Planning a cheap week of meals means researching store deals and combining multiple recipes, which takes time and effort. Easy Eats does that for you.
- **Solution:** Easy Eats puts recipes, budgets, deals, and cooking lessons in one place. A typical week set up looks like this:
    1. The user adjusts their profile according to household size, dietary needs, favourite stores, and what is in their pantry for that week.
    2. They pick a budget, and the app builds a one-week meal plan and grocery list that fits it. If the meal plan is over budget, it tells the user and provides a cross-store comparison of the grocery stores available to them.
    3. While cooking, they ask the AI chat for swaps ("no sugar? Use honey") and can refer to the tutorials throughout.
    4. They rate each meal, and the next plan adjusts accordingly.
- **Revenue:** Easy Eats earns revenue in three ways: a free plan that brings users in, a $2.99/month premium plan, and fees from grocery partners.

## 2. Where IT creates value
Easy Eats changes the value chain for home cooking. Today, finding a recipe means searching the internet, scrolling past ads and long stories on food blogs, and piecing together YouTube videos when a step is unclear. Easy Eats replaces those separate tasks with one app. In operations, AI turns a tested recipe library into plans that fit each user's budget, diet, household size, and kitchen. In service, users can get help while they cook through a swap chat for missing ingredients, step-by-step tutorials, and a skill tracker that builds confidence. Outbound, recipes arrive in one clear format instead of being scattered across websites. Similarly, store deals appear inside meal plans instead of in paper flyers or across multiple different grocery store apps, and grocery stores can pay Easy Eats to promote their offers per week. Switching costs will grow as more and more users build their pantry, profile, and skill history. Once users have an established weekly recipe rotation, the convenience and weeks of planning would make it too much work to switch to another app. Differentiation comes from combining recipes, lessons, and budgets that competitors offer separately. As of right now, Easy Eats is a static website hosted online making it a low-cost distribution channel that can scale to reach many users. A shared cloud backend would be needed before it could scale with connected accounts and community data.

## 3. Network effects
Every new user makes Easy Eats more useful for the next one. Community ratings and swap reviews ("honey instead of sugar worked for this recipe," 4.6 stars from 312 other cooks) become more trustworthy as more people cook. Shared household lists bring in roommates and partners, and once a whole household is on the app, nobody will want to switch. Easy Eats is primarily a one-sided market where users help each other with ratings. However, with enough local shoppers, Easy Eats becomes a two-sided market: grocery stores pay to reach them, which makes the app more useful and attracts more users.

Every new user adds ratings, swap results, and price reports, so recipes, swaps, and deals get better for everyone. Once there are enough users, grocery stores pay the app to reach them, turning a one-sided network into a two-sided one. That being said, Easy Eats is useful even with zero other users because meal plans, My Pantry, the swap chat, and tutorials work for one person. It would launch in one small community (StFX, Antigonish, NS), with seeded recipes and flyer prices. However, users are what make it unique. Early users could earn free premium weeks by rating meals and reporting prices. Each recipe card shows a community rating (for example, "4.6 stars from 312 cooks"), the swap chat shows how many cooks liked each swap, and the Grocery Planner has a "report a price" button. The community numbers are sample data that show how the network would work at a proper scale.

## 4. Can it be copied? (the moat question)
If I could build this website in a few days, anybody can. The advantage is not in the code, but what users contribute: ratings, swap results, price reports, taste history, and dietary needs. This grows with every user and every week, meaning a copycat would start at zero. Easy Eats' most valuable resource is its community data, making recipes and deals better with every day that users use it. It is rare because no competitor collects this specifically for local home cooks, and it is hard to imitate because it takes months of real user activity to build. The features alone are easy to copy, but the data and local community are not. Many apps can perform one task Easy Eats offers, but users would have to use several apps for separate actions, making Easy Eats hard to substitute because it combines them all in one place. A company like Mealime or Flipp that currently focuses on one task could copy the features in a few weeks. To truly catch up, it would also need thousands of local ratings, months of price history in each town and city users operate from, and households willing to leave Easy Eats, which would take at least a year per community. Data grows with every meal rated and price reported. The network grows as each new user makes the app better and increases accuracy for the next. Switching costs rise as users build their pantry, profile, saved recipes, and skill progress. The brand strengthens as Easy Eats becomes the local name for cooking on a budget.

## 5. Cloud and operations

 A real version would need cloud services for accounts, databases, payments, AI, and shared community data. Easy Eats would need a small team made up of a founder, developer, and someone to test recipes and grow the community. The prototype's sample data would be replaced with a real database, user accounts, payments, an AI model for the swap chat, and current store prices. Since stores may not share their data, prices would come from manual search of weekly flyers and user reports at first. For SaaS, I would include payment software for the $2.99/month subscriptions and Google Maps for the store finder. The AI chat would use an existing AI model through an API because developing and training our own would be too expensive. For PaaS, Easy Eats would need a managed platform to run the code and central database. Right now, ratings, pantry items, and kitchen items are saved in local storage, meaning they stay in the user's individual browser. As a result, users cannot currently sync across devices or share data with roommates, partners, or families. A central database would fix this and allow community ratings and price reports to build over time. I would not use IaaS at launch because, with a small community, there would not be enough staff or budget to set up, patch, and rent servers. There are a couple of risks when relying on these services:

 1. If our PaaS provider went down, logins, synced pantries, household lists, price reports, and store comparisons would stop working. Users' recipes and latest grocery list would have to be saved on their device so that they can continue to cook, plan, and shop offline.
 2. The AI could suggest an unsafe swap for someone with an allergy; unsafe ingredients would have to be blocked using the user's dietary profile, show allergy warnings, and instruct users to check labels.

## 6. Risks and ethics

There are a few risks involved. To start, the AI could suggest a swap that triggers an allergy or give unclear steps that leave, for example, chicken undercooked. Calorie and weight goals could contribute to unhealthy habits. Easy Eats would have to block ingredients that conflict with a user's dietary profile, add safe cooking temperatures to every meat recipe, and allow users to hide calorie counts. Diet, health goals, religion (halal), and location are sensitive information. A breach or sale of this data to stores would break users' trust. Easy Eats would never sell personal data, give stores only anonymous totals, collect only what it needs, and let users delete their account and data at any time, following applicable Canadian privacy laws such as PIPEDA. Paid store deals could push shoppers toward less healthy or pricier food, and fake price reports could mislead users. Small local stores could also be left out if only big chains pay. Easy Eats would label every sponsored deal, never let payments change a user's budget or recommendations, flag price reports that look wrong, and list small local stores for free.
